Finding prompt injections
at web scale.

Building the data infrastructure for AI applications that keep learning and improving, affordably and at scale.

MLHouse · In collaboration with Ermes Cyber Security S.p.A.14 September 2026

Abstract

MLHouse is building the data infrastructure that enables AI applications to keep learning and improving, affordably and at scale. Prompt-injection discovery is our case study: keeping detectors up to date requires finding new attack patterns in large, changing datasets.

A five-million-page Common Crawl investigation, a separate expanded-pattern scan, and controlled embedding experiments lead to three practical lessons.

Three lessons from prompt-injection discovery

  1. Balance cost, speed, and discovery quality.Lexical search is economical for known wording; model analysis adds context at greater cost. Scaling requires choosing where additional computation is worthwhile.
  2. Adapt and evaluate models for the task.The tested classifiers produced large review queues and missed reference cases. Fine-tuning embeddings improved exact-payload retrieval from 1 to 46 successful queries out of 106 against a million-page background. Generalisation to unknown attacks remains unresolved.
  3. Make improvement a continuing cycle.An evolving threat motivates repeated discovery, validation, training, and evaluation. Retained data and reusable indexes provide the foundation for those updates.

These lessons motivate an automated prompt-injection discovery and learning workflow. The experiments establish requirements and promising components; demonstrating the complete workflow's cost and quality remains the next step.

01 / Context

AI infrastructure needs more than one way to access and use data.

AI applications that improve over time need a continuing supply of useful evidence: data they can revisit with new questions, examples they can learn from, and evaluations that distinguish progress from regression. MLHouse is building the data foundation for that cycle. We expect this need to recur across many demanding AI applications, with prompt-injection discovery providing a concrete test of the requirements.

The target lakehouse integrates large datasets with language models through full scans, SQL queries and aggregations, full-text search, vector search with different embeddings, and approximate query methods. These access paths serve different tasks over shared, versioned data. The system must support autonomous AI agents as well as human users: agents can repeatedly search, inspect, aggregate, and revisit data within a single workflow. As the volume and variety of requests expand, flexibility has to come with control over compute, storage, latency, and result quality.

A lakehouse that adapts to its workload

At terabyte scale and beyond, building and maintaining every possible representation of every record can consume a large budget. Our research goal is to choose which parts of the data justify full-text indexes, which need vector representations, which benefit from summaries or precomputed aggregates, and which can be scanned or sampled when needed. The choice should reflect expected reuse, construction and maintenance costs, and the task's quality and freshness requirements. Several access methods may contribute to the same request.

The proposed architecture closes a feedback loop around those decisions. People and agents generate workloads; the system records access patterns, resource use, latency, and available quality feedback. A learning component uses that evidence to propose changes to data organization, representations, and query execution. Changes are evaluated against workload requirements before adoption, and their effects inform the next round of decisions.

Prompt-injection discovery, investigated with Ermes Cyber Security S.p.A., gives this infrastructure work a concrete challenge. Relevant passages can be short, multilingual, repetitive, or obscured by unrelated page content. Many benign pages discuss the same language as attacks. The collection is large enough that repeatedly reading every page with a capable model becomes expensive.

The case study follows three questions: how to make repeated discovery affordable, how to adapt models that miss useful evidence, and how to turn new findings into evaluated improvements. The experiments measure parts of that process. They guide the next stage: connecting discovery and detector learning to a lakehouse that can adapt how it serves the workload.

02 / Motivation

Continuous discovery makes repeated scans expensive.

Building a detection system requires more than training on the attack patterns already in hand. We need to keep looking for emerging instructions, unfamiliar variants, and benign lookalikes, then use reviewed examples to improve the detector. This is why we want an indexed data lake: it lets us revisit an evolving collection with new questions and search for patterns we did not know to look for when the data was collected.

The intended learning cycle is ingest and index → discover candidates → review and label → retrain → evaluate. Every round consumes resources, so the first lesson is to balance cost, speed, and discovery quality across the cycle. A fast query that misses new patterns and an exhaustive model pass that exceeds the budget both limit what the system can learn. Source context, versioned labels, and independent evaluations let us measure whether the work produces useful improvements.

Why one search method is not enough

Full-text search is the natural baseline for known wording. Once an index is built, new word and phrase queries can revisit the collection without running a language model over every document. Rules and regular expressions capture known signatures. The difficulty is specifying what to search for: an emerging instruction may use wording we have never observed, and listing every paraphrase, language, and combination of terms is impractical.

Task-tuned embeddings offer a way to search for related instructions. The aim is to bring passages with similar attack behavior closer together despite differences in wording or surrounding content. A vector index makes those representations searchable at scale. This motivates our fine-tuning experiments, but broad transfer across languages and unfamiliar attack families remains to be demonstrated.

Obfuscation can challenge both approaches. Encoded text or altered characters may obscure the signal from lexical queries and embedding models alike. It is a separate problem from ordinary multilingual or paraphrase variation. The proposed approach combines original and normalized representations, complementary retrieval methods, and selective model review; the held-out encoding experiment illustrates this limitation in the tested model.

What would an LLM pass over Common Crawl cost?

We use 2.10 billion page inspections, matching the approximate page count of the June 2026 Common Crawl release, CC-MAIN-2026-25. This gives us a concrete workload tied to the crawl used in our investigation. The archive count is a planning proxy for extracted-text inspections; it does not establish that every archived page has an eligible text record. [4]

The measured pipeline applies an LLM to every page. Its first pass uses gpt-oss-20b with input capped at 8,000 characters, low reasoning effort, and up to 512 generated tokens. It uses batched inference and prefix caching, with one model replica per H100 GPU. Only the 5,823 candidates out of five million pages (0.116%) receive the more detailed review by both gpt-oss-20b and gpt-oss-120b. This staged strategy concentrates the larger model's work on a small candidate set.

For a more expensive alternative, we also project applying the gpt-oss-120b review procedure to every page. That procedure uses four H100 GPUs, a different rubric, a larger input allowance, and up to 3,072 generated tokens. Its measured candidate-review throughput provides a basis for a cost scenario, although we have not run it over an unfiltered crawl.

Existing staged pipeline. The five-million-page investigation consumed 89.3703 allocated GPU-hours across first-pass attempts and two final adjudications, giving 17.8741 per million pages. This includes unsuccessful first-pass work and allocation overhead, but excludes the classifier baseline and other exploratory experiments. Generation logs support a processing-time estimate of approximately 11.2 GPU-hours per million, excluding overhead and combining a 500,000-page first-pass run with the five-million-page investigation's candidate reviews. The projection assumes the same candidate fraction.

120B review on every page. Reviewing 5,823 candidates took 1,049.2 seconds of model processing on four GPUs: 1.1658 GPU-hours, or approximately 200.2 GPU-hours per million pages. The full job occupied those four GPUs for 1,672 seconds: 1.8578 GPU-hours, or approximately 319.0 per million. Multiplying these rates by 2,100 gives the second row. This scenario replaces the staged pipeline with a single 120B review of each page; it does not add the earlier first pass or the separate 20B review. Calculations use unrounded measured values before rounding the displayed results.

The 120B projection extrapolates from selected candidate pages, whose lengths and reasoning demands may differ from the rest of the crawl. The allocated-time version also scales up the overhead of a small job; that overhead may amortize differently in a sustained scan. The difference between strategies includes their prompts, context limits, decoding budgets, and parallelism, so it cannot be attributed to model size alone or treated as a measured saving at equal detection quality.

Neither scenario measures the most expensive possible LLM scan. Both use capped inputs; longer documents, more reasoning, repeated judgments, or a different model would require a separate estimate. Crawling, network transfer, persistent storage, indexing, human review, and taxes are outside these compute figures. The existing workflow also misses cases, so its cost does not buy exhaustive detection.

Reuse preparation across discovery rounds

Retaining and indexing data lets new hypotheses become new queries over the same collection. Expensive model analysis can then focus on promising candidates, while sampled inspection probes regions that lexical and vector retrieval both miss. Reviewed results supply examples for training and reveal what to search for next.

The economic question is how much additional validated content each method finds within a budget and a useful response time. Full-text index construction, embedding computation, vector indexing, ingestion, and later re-embedding all consume resources. Measure these costs alongside verification and review over repeated searches. Reuse may make preparation worthwhile, but building every representation of every record is itself a strategy to test.

The goal is to keep learning from emerging patterns without paying for a complete model-based reread of the corpus at every iteration.

03 / Study design

Measure discovery, model quality, and the cost of learning.

The study examines the components needed for a continuing learning workflow. Broad model inspection establishes candidate examples and a compute baseline. Lexical and classifier experiments test inexpensive ways to select candidates. Controlled embedding experiments test whether training changes retrieval quality. The populations below answer different questions; they do not form one benchmark on which all methods can be ranked.

We use AI-directed content for text addressed to an assistant or automated reader. An injection attempt asks the system to treat instructions from that external content as authority over its task. A quoted prompt, a security tutorial, and an ordinary routing notice can resemble an attack without being one. Motive is a separate dimension: an instruction may seek promotion, obstruction, or harmful action.

The experiments examine extracted page text, candidate selection, and controlled retrieval. They do not demonstrate that a deployed agent followed an instruction, leaked information, or performed an unauthorized action. They also do not yet measure improvements across successive detector updates. Attack effectiveness and the complete learning cycle each require a separate evaluation.

What the models actually see

Common Crawl WET records provide extracted text, rather than the original HTML, headers, or rendered page. The five-million-page census reads raw WET files, but first-pass model inputs are still capped at 8,000 characters and fitted to a token budget. The logs show input truncation for 1,019,689 records, or 20.39%. Candidate adjudication returns to the source text with a larger, token-based allowance. The earlier indexed text surface was capped at 4,000 characters. Access to raw WET therefore improves available coverage without guaranteeing full-page inspection.

The census samples files and takes the first eligible records from each selected file, excluding very short text. This is not a uniform random sample of web pages. The second judge sees first-pass candidates only, so it cannot identify cases the first pass missed. The run also records 185 errors among the five million verdict records.

Position within existing research

Large-scale observation of web injections is already an active research area. Google's April 2026 study uses Common Crawl to investigate real-world instructions and their objectives. Khodayari and colleagues examine webpage and HTTP-response injections and separately test model behavior. Our contribution is to examine discovery as a recurring data workload: the cost of inspecting content, the limitations of candidate selection, and the evidence needed to improve retrieval and detection over time. 04 / Observations

Instructions aimed at machines are already in the corpus.

The observed instructions show why discovery needs both broad coverage and careful review. They span promotion, attempts to obstruct automated reading, and harmful requests. Similar wording also appears in benign demonstrations and published prompts. Those distinctions matter when discovered pages become training examples.

The five-million-page census produced 5,823 first-pass candidates. Adjudication confirmed 456 as AI-directed with the 20B judge and 375 with the 120B judge; 248 were shared. Agreement across candidate verdicts was imperfect, with Cohen's κ of 0.566.

The subsequent genre classifier selected 61 adversarial pages across 15 domains from those shared confirmations. Full-source review found three clear contextual false positives: a published model execution log, a security explainer, and a displayed red-team demonstration. A fourth page is a routing/attribution boundary case. Excluding all four leaves a proposed 57-page, 11-domain set under a broad convention that includes output-targeted promotion and anti-crawler overrides. This is a provisional review result, not a released, independently adjudicated gold standard.

What the instructions actually say

These short, verbatim excerpts reproduce page text captured in CC-MAIN-2026-25. The first four illustrate instructions retained under the broad review definition; the fifth was excluded after checking context. We observed the text, not whether an agent followed it. The provisional grouping contains 29 promotion, 27 deterrence, and one harmful-command page. Counts are concentrated by site and template: page totals should not be read as independent attack campaigns. More importantly, agreement between two model judges did not remove the need to examine original context.

Expanded patterns surfaced additional domains

A separate scan extended the earlier 31-rule baseline. Catalog reconstruction generated 32 patterns, then two revision rounds expanded the set to 44 and 57. The scan reports 64,765 raw hits across 51,128,154 pages. The selected review queue contains 1,520 hits from nine rules, covering 350 domains. Every domain in that selected group has a review label: 75 positive and 275 negative, with 68 of the positives marked absent from the census set.

Read the denominator carefully. The 75/350 result is the yield of a selected domain-review queue. Another 63,245 raw hits from six noisy rules were not triaged. The full raw-hit output and scan logs were unavailable for verification, so the full-scan totals remain reported rather than independently recomputed. “68 additional domains” does not mean 68 attacks missed by the census on the same pages.

Revising the patterns illustrates one part of the intended feedback cycle: observed examples and misses guide new searches, and those searches produce candidates for review. The large negative and untriaged queues show why finding more matches is only an intermediate result. The useful output is additional, reliably labeled evidence that justifies the search and review effort.

Evidence of growth, with incomplete visibility

Independent monitoring provides evidence of increased activity. Google reports a 32% relative increase in malicious-category detections between November 2025 and February 2026 across repeated Common Crawl scans. Its researchers also expect the scale and sophistication of attempts to grow, as more capable AI systems become valuable targets and automation lowers the cost of attacking them. The measured increase and the expectation of future growth are distinct claims. [1]

Our historical pattern scans reported zero confirmed adversarial cases among approximately 1.31 billion pages from 2024, compared with 21 among 14.6 million pages from 2026. These reported counts have not been independently reproduced. The contrast is consistent with increased activity, but differences in crawl populations, text coverage, sampling, and review prevent a controlled growth estimate. Zero detections in the earlier scan does not establish that attacks were absent.

Continued growth and diversification are reasonable planning assumptions; an exponential trend has not been established. Our detection experiments also miss known examples, so the observed cases form an incomplete inventory. The number of undiscovered attacks remains unknown. Together, these findings motivate continuous discovery and repeated evaluation as the threat changes.

The inspected Ermes Cyber Security S.p.A. export produced two initial claims and zero confirmations. That is a result for this dataset and pipeline, rather than proof that the wider source population contains no attacks.

05 / Detection quality

The tested models need adaptation and independent evaluation.

The second lesson is that choosing a pretrained model does not settle the discovery problem. On this workload, the two tested classifiers leave substantial review work and miss reference cases. Language-model inspection also misses known examples and misreads some benign context. These findings motivate task-specific training and evaluation; they do not establish that all open-source classifiers behave the same way.

The tested classifiers create substantial review work

At a 0.5 threshold on 500,000 pages, ProtectAI flagged 141,993 pages (28.40%) and PIGuard flagged 49,786 (9.96%). They recovered 6 and 10 of the 20 judge-selected reference cases respectively. Both leave a large queue for further analysis while missing examples already surfaced by the census.

The reference cases were discovered by the judge, not drawn from an independent complete attack set. Recovery here is conditional on those cases. Classifier-only hits were not comprehensively reviewed, so the flagged fractions are neither false-positive rates nor estimates of attack prevalence.

Patterns are useful, but the evaluation population matters

On the 4,014-row calibration benchmark, a normalized 40-term keyword detector recovers 418 of 2,003 positive rows (20.87%), with six negative rows flagged. Without normalization it recovers 257 positives (12.83%). On the same labeled benchmark, the census pipeline recovers 1,120 positives (55.92%). The language-model pipeline therefore has substantial misses even in this controlled setting.

These 40 keywords are a different detector from the structural regex set. Reapplying the expanded 57 patterns to the saved quote/evidence fields matches 276 of 456 positive 20B judgments (60.5%). Because those patterns were revised using observed misses, that result measures adaptation on the inspected data. It is not held-out, full-page attack recall.

Language models add context, but are not ground truth

The census can surface wording outside the fixed keyword vocabulary. Its remaining calibration misses, judge disagreement, and quoted-example false positives show why model outputs need source-context review before becoming training labels. An independent sample is also necessary to investigate what every candidate-selection method misses. Otherwise, a learning loop risks reinforcing its own blind spots.

Evaluate the complete candidate path

Synthetic embedding probes report ROC-AUC around 0.927 for a linear probe and about 0.94 for an MLP. A separate retrieval experiment reports 386/400 queries finding any planted chunk in their top 100. Probe discrimination, retrieval success, and detector precision measure different things. These results do not establish the precision or attack-document recall of a combined discovery pipeline.

A filter changes the population passed to the next model. Its downstream classifier must therefore be evaluated on the actual candidates, including their benign lookalikes. The practical goal is a measured path from retrieval to reviewed labels, with both missed cases and review effort counted. The following experiment tests whether adapting the embedding representation can improve one component of that path.

06 / Learned retrieval

Fine-tuning changes what the retriever pays attention to.

A vector index makes embedding representations searchable; the representation determines which passages appear similar. The experiment here tests adapting that representation to injection-like content. Weak base-model results concern the tested embeddings and retrieval task, rather than vector indexing as a technology.

We fine-tune BGE-M3 on synthetic injections. A deliberate overfit setting with shared payloads across different host pages is kept separate from an evaluation with disjoint payload text. The generalisation run uses 6,295 training pairs. Exact selected web-derived payloads and evaluation host IDs are absent from its training split.

For the web-derived evaluation, payloads are inserted into new clean host pages. The payload text itself becomes the query. Set A contains 106 usable records selected from AI-directed judgments whose quote/evidence text does not match the expanded patterns. Set B contains 36 selected representatives from the combined census and pattern-derived collection. Each set is searched separately against its injected targets plus one million clean distractor pages.

Task-specific training substantially improves this controlled retrieval result, supporting further work on adapted representations. It does not yet show that tuned embeddings discover unknown attacks better than lexical search. Three features of the evaluation define that limit.

  1. The 106 records are not a consistently validated attack set.The builder starts from all 456 positive 20B judgments rather than the adversarial subset. Matching the selected records to the earlier genre output gives 99 other-AI-directed, two marketing-to-AI, and five adversarial records. Those genre labels are imperfect too; this is a taxonomy mismatch, not a replacement gold count.
  2. The query already contains the target's payload.Every Set A and Set B query is a literal substring of its constructed page. A matched literal-search or BM25 baseline is needed to establish the benefit beyond full-text retrieval. Discovering unknown instructions requires queries defined before seeing the targets and evaluation in their original context.
  3. Payload separation is not template-family separation.The synthetic split has no exact payload overlap, but 160 template families are shared. Of 1,618 evaluation rows, 1,593 (98.45%) belong to families seen during training. The result supports transfer to held-out variants under this split; unseen-family generalisation remains an open question.

Obfuscation tests the representation as well as the retriever

A paraphrase or translation changes wording while preserving an instruction's meaning. Obfuscation can also change how that instruction is represented, through character substitutions, invisible characters, or encoded text. Semantic similarity alone does not guarantee that the embedding model will recover the underlying instruction.

The held-out base64 result makes this limitation concrete. When base64 wrapping is excluded from training, the saved evaluation reports recall@1 moving only from 0.1% to 0.2%. This particular result shows little transfer to the held-out encoding. The improvement in exact-payload retrieval therefore does not establish general resistance to obfuscation.

A proposed extension is to retain the original text alongside normalized or decoded views where applicable, then compare lexical and learned retrieval over those representations. Original context remains necessary to distinguish an attack from benign encoded content or a quoted example. Evaluation should report paraphrase, language, character-variation, and encoding holdouts separately, including the additional computation and false positives introduced by each transformation.

The implication for continuous learning is concrete: representation updates need tests of what they recover, what they still miss, and whether the improvement warrants rebuilding affected indexes. A gain on familiar variants cannot substitute for evaluation on new attack families and forms of obfuscation.

Reproducibility limits

Retrieval scores were recomputed from per-query ranks; GPU training and inference were not rerun for this analysis. The archived training and evaluation code differs from the recorded job configuration, so the exact executed versions still need to be reconciled before independent reproduction is possible. The recorded training-pair count is consistent with the stated generalisation split; the version mismatch does not establish that training used the wrong split.

07 / Infrastructure implications

Support the whole learning cycle in the data layer.

The third lesson connects the findings to MLHouse's broader mission: improvement requires infrastructure that makes new evidence usable, repeatedly. Discovery needs complementary access methods; model adaptation needs trustworthy examples; every update needs independent evaluation. A shared lakehouse can retain the source records, representations, labels, and versions that connect those activities.

In the proposed workflow, lexical queries exploit known signatures, learned representations contribute candidates for related instructions, and sampled model inspection investigates regions retrieval misses. Source-context review establishes labels. Training and evaluation then determine whether those examples improve the detector or embedding model. Validated updates change the next round of discovery.

Choose representations according to the work they enable

The data layer must retain source context alongside searchable representations, connect labels and transformations to their original records, and track model and index versions. It must also expose cost, latency, and coverage across the full query path. Scans and SQL analytics can describe the collection and its gaps; lexical and vector indexes retrieve candidates; summaries and approximate methods can reduce work where the required quality permits it.

Fine-tuning changes the lifecycle of an index. A useful new representation may justify re-encoding selected data. Other subsets may be better served by an existing text index, a summary, a sample, or an occasional full scan. Index reuse and incremental refresh should be evaluated across successive requests, including the cost of maintaining each representation. This is the architectural response to the cost-quality trade-off: select and revise the investment in data access as its expected use changes.

Two learning loops, with different objectives

Detector learning uses reviewed examples to improve recognition of attacks. Infrastructure learning uses observed workloads and their outcomes to improve how the lakehouse organizes and serves data. The first changes what we are trying to find; the second changes where and how we spend resources finding it. Connecting them would allow new discovery needs to influence which data is indexed, embedded, summarized, or inspected approximately.

That connection is a research objective. It requires measuring the cost of creating and refreshing representations alongside the benefits they deliver over subsequent agent requests. A useful policy must adapt as demand changes while respecting quality and freshness targets, including when the evidence favors a full scan or retaining an existing index.

Normalize the economics before claiming savings

Extrapolating the embedding experiment's reported throughput gives approximately 21 GPU-hours to encode 51 million pages. At the same page count, the language-model processing estimate is approximately 571 GPU-hours. Encoding alone omits index construction, retrieval, verification, and review. This comparison therefore concerns projected components, not complete systems or measured end-to-end savings.

Future runs should report allocated GPU-hours, CPU and storage/index costs, and reviewer time separately. The dollar scenarios in Section 2 use a commercial GPU price to illustrate the scale of the workload; they do not convert RES billing units into money.

08 / Research agenda

Build the discovery and learning cycle.

The next stage is to develop a prompt-injection detection system supported by automated discovery, training, and evaluation. It should repeatedly collect and index data, surface candidates through complementary retrieval methods, establish reliable labels with appropriate review, test model updates, and refresh detectors and retrieval representations when improvements are validated. The MLHouse architecture is intended to supply the reusable data and access methods for this cycle and adapt how it serves the changing workload.

The immediate experiment is concrete: at a fixed budget, does adding learned retrieval find useful cases beyond an improved lexical baseline? The following steps connect that measurement to the target system.

  1. Freeze a common evaluation population.Use the same source records, text surfaces, and coverage limits for each method. Record page and domain counts, exclude known demonstrations, and have multiple reviewers resolve ambiguous categories. Track classifier-only and retrieval-only candidates as well as judge-selected cases.
  2. Compare methods under matched conditions.Run structural patterns, normalized full-text/BM25 retrieval, base embeddings, tuned embeddings, and selective model verification. Separate known-payload lookup from discovery queries fixed before evaluation. Report precision, recovery, and incremental reviewed cases by method.
  3. Strengthen the holdouts.Separate payloads, template families, domains, and provenance where possible. Test original pages as well as spliced targets. Keep the deliberate overfit experiment distinct from tests of unseen families. Measure paraphrases, languages, character variations, and encodings separately; compare original text with normalized or decoded views to identify which transformations improve discovery.
  4. Measure the cost-quality curve.Vary candidate budgets and verification depth. Count all encoding and indexing work, unsuccessful runs, and review time. Measure additional validated cases per GPU-hour and per reviewer-hour, together with coverage on an independently reviewed holdout.
  5. Test successive learning rounds.Add newly reviewed examples to training, update detectors and representations, and evaluate each version on independent holdouts and later data. Measure new discoveries, regressions, review effort, training cost, and index-refresh cost. Adopt updates only when they meet the application's quality and resource targets.
  6. Evaluate adaptation to changing workloads.Replay changing agent workloads and compare fixed index configurations with policies that selectively build or refresh text indexes, embeddings, summaries, and samples. Include construction and maintenance costs over the full sequence of requests, and evaluate quality, latency, and freshness before adopting a change.
  7. Make updates reproducible.Archive the executed code, patterns, input manifests, labels, splits, models, and result tables. Reuse that protocol for future crawls before drawing conclusions about growth.

The next milestone is a repeatable learning cycle that finds additional validated cases within a measured budget.

Success requires evidence that the system improves across successive updates, with the costs of discovery, review, training, and data preparation included. That is the bridge from the present experiments to an effective continuous-learning application.

09 / Conclusions

Conclusions and limits of the evidence.

The findings support three priorities: spend expensive analysis where it adds useful evidence, adapt and evaluate the models used to select that evidence, and build a repeatable process for turning reviewed findings into improvements. The observed instructions and missed reference cases make the need concrete. The fine-tuning result shows that a representation can improve for a defined task; the obfuscation result shows how much remains to be tested.

The evidence establishes different candidate yields, substantial review workloads, and a controlled retrieval improvement recomputed from saved ranks. It does not establish global attack prevalence, a growth rate from our own scans, demonstrated agent compromise, or a validated cost advantage for the complete proposed system. Google's separate monitoring supports increased detections and the expectation of further growth, without establishing an exponential trend. [1]

Results are limited by sampling, repeated sites and templates, incomplete text coverage, model-selected reference sets, and unresolved annotation boundaries. The 57-page review proposal and the 75 positive-domain pattern labels use broad definitions that still need a shared adjudication policy. They should not be added together as independent counts of unique attacks.

Full reproducibility requires the complete pattern-scan output and logs, the exact executed generalisation scripts, reconciled labels, and a consistent cost ledger. Matched lexical comparisons and stronger holdouts require additional experiments. These remain part of the next research phase.

The central takeaway

Keeping detectors up to date requires continuously discovering new attack patterns, and making that discovery affordable is a data-infrastructure problem. Our findings motivate complementary access methods, adaptation of the tested models, and a continuing cycle of reviewed evidence and evaluated updates. Prompt injection makes these requirements concrete; we expect the same infrastructure needs to recur across many demanding AI applications.

MLHouse is building the data infrastructure that enables AI applications to keep learning and improving, affordably and at scale.
